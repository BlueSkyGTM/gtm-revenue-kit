# Competitor Radar — White-Label Campaign

*The alternative set for the White-Label buyer (accounting/CPA/bookkeeping firms buying delivery
capacity). Separate on purpose from `context/competitor-radar.md` (the Bounce radar): a firm
deciding who does its overflow bookkeeping is not choosing between Fenton and QuickBooks Live or
Bench — those are the SMB's alternatives, never this buyer's. Built by
`playbooks/impact-positioning.md` step 1. Never load both radars in one session.*

Last updated: 2026-07-27

---

## Status Quo (the partners absorb it)

**One-line summary:** Not a company — the default is the firm keeping the overflow: partners and
senior staff doing reconciliations at night, books slipping behind between deadlines.
**Pricing:** Invisible — paid in partner hours billed at nothing against a 3–5x advisory rate.
**Typical buyer:** Every firm on the list until a commitment visibly breaks.

**Their strengths (be honest):**
- Zero trust risk — no outsider ever touches a client file
- No procurement, no vendor management, no new cost line
- Quality control is implicit: the reviewer is the doer

**Their weaknesses:**
- The overflow lands in the same weeks as publicly promised closes (observed: a WL-1 firm
  advertising "100% on-time delivery" across 40+ books while separately selling cleanup —
  lumpy, unscheduled work colliding with a guaranteed calendar)
- The cost is the partner's realized rate, which never appears on a P&L
- It does not scale: the ceiling is the partner's own hours, and the constraint the site visits
  actually verified is **time against a commitment** — not exhaustion

**Why firms switch to us:** a month where the commitment nearly broke — a cleanup landed during
close, a staff departure, a client noticing books three months behind and starting to shop.

**How to handle "we handle overflow ourselves":**
> Ask what happened the last time a cleanup landed in a close week, and what the partner-hours on
> it would have billed at the firm's advisory rate. Their own number does the selling.

## In-house hiring (the staff bookkeeper)

**One-line summary:** The "real" fix every firm considers first: hire a staff bookkeeper or
staff accountant.
**Pricing:** $45–65K salary + benefits + management overhead, carried year-round against
seasonal peaks.
**Typical buyer:** Firms whose overflow has become chronic rather than episodic.

**Their strengths (be honest):**
- Full-time availability, in the firm's own systems and culture
- Compounds: a good hire gets better every year and the knowledge stays
- The firm's clients never know anyone external exists

**Their weaknesses:**
- Slow (months to hire) and risky (a bad hire in a small firm is expensive twice)
- Year-round fixed cost against lumpy demand — the February resignation scenario runs the
  other way too: the hire idles in the trough
- Active hiring is itself the capacity signal the ICP keys on: a firm posting for a bookkeeper
  is publicly announcing months of unmet delivery

**Why firms switch to us:** the math — a standing retainer plus per-file work flexes with actual
volume and disappears when not needed; a salary does not.

**How to handle "I could just hire":**
> Agree — for chronic, full-time volume, they should. The retainer is for the other shape of the
> problem: episodic, high-stakes work that cannot justify a seat but cannot safely wait for one.

## Offshore / outsourced delivery arms

**One-line summary:** The established outsourcing industry (offshore teams and domestic BPO-style
providers) selling bookkeeping hours to firms at commodity rates.
**Pricing:** Commodity hourly, often far below any US practitioner rate. On price, we lose —
this is written down as a hard loss condition, not fought.
**Typical buyer:** Volume-oriented firms optimizing unit economics; also the source of most
"I tried outsourcing and got burned" stories on the list.

**Their strengths (be honest):**
- Price, decisively, and elastic capacity at any volume
- Established playbooks, security attestations, references at scale

**Their weaknesses:**
- Anonymous and rotating: every file arrives cold, the re-briefing overhead never goes away —
  the structural reason temp labor stays cheap
- Rework risk lands on the partner's review hours, which is the exact scarce resource the firm
  was trying to protect
- A firm with an established offshore arm has already solved its capacity problem — the ICP
  correctly excludes them (anti-ICP row, not a target)

**Why firms switch to us:** the work is **episodic, high-stakes, and credentialed** — a cleanup
or migration file they cannot hand to a queue. One named 30-year ProAdvisor whose work survives
partner review, warm on the firm's context because the retainer already paid the briefing cost.

**How to handle "offshore quotes a third of your rate":**
> Don't contest the rate — concede it. The retainer is not selling hours; it is selling
> readiness: warm context and a guaranteed start window in the month a file actually lands.
> An offshore hour is cheap because it arrives cold. Walk away from a pure rate-card comparison.

## Other domestic solo/boutique overflow providers

**One-line summary:** Freelance bookkeepers and other small practices selling overflow or
cleanup capacity to firms — the closest structural twins, and the source of the market's price
comps.
**Pricing:** Observed public comp: $125/hr for historical cleanup (a WL-1 competitor's published
rate card); retainers from low hundreds per month.
**Typical buyer:** Firms that already believe in outsourced delivery and are choosing a person.

**Their strengths (be honest):**
- Same trust shape as Fenton: a named person, US-based, reviewable work
- Some carry the proof points Fenton lacks — live references, published rate cards, existing
  firm relationships

**Their weaknesses:**
- Most sell loose hours, not a standing relationship — no capacity guarantee when a file lands
  in the worst week
- Rarely credentialed at the 30-year ProAdvisor level; the $125 public comp belongs to a
  provider *without* that credential, which is why the comp is a floor, not a peg

**Why firms choose us:** the credential plus the structure — a retainer that guarantees a start
window, versus a freelancer's best effort. **[PROOF GAP]** no delivered engagement yet backs
this; until one exists, the credential and the structure are the whole argument.

## Referral-out (the non-competitor exit)

**One-line summary:** Firms that do not run monthly books at all simply refer the work away
(observed on WL-1: setup-and-coach practices that publicly connect clients to outside CPAs).
**Why it matters:** these firms are not buyers of capacity and never will be — they are a
**referral channel**, with the poaching objection pre-answered because routing clients elsewhere
is already their business. Segment C of the WL-1 re-segmentation; treat as partners, not
prospects, and never send them the capacity pitch.

---

## What is deliberately absent

Bench, QuickBooks Live, and Intuit do not appear on this radar. They compete for the *end
client's* books, not for the *firm's* delivery capacity — they are the Bounce buyer's
alternatives (`context/competitor-radar.md`). If a message to a firm ever leans on Bench's
shutdown or QB Live's backlog, the two sets have been mixed and the send is wrong.
