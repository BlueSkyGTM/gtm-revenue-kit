# White Label List 1 — market read: overflow QuickBooks work

*WL-1 = `Contacts WL-1` (`tblafsL94yxtXp9fa`), 617 records. Top 20 by score visited individually with `/browse` on 2026-07-26. Per-account briefs for four of them at `outputs/2026-07-26-*-research.md`.*

**Question being answered:** is there a market here for white-label overflow QuickBooks work, and what shape is it?

**Short answer: yes, but the list is four different markets wearing the same clothes, and only one of them is the overflow buyer. Roughly a quarter of the top 20 fits the thesis cleanly.**

---

## 1. The list decays faster than the score suggests

Three of the top 20 are unusable, and **all three scored 90 or 100**:

| Account | Scored | What the site showed |
|---|---|---|
| Midwest Bookkeeping (Melissa Downs) | 90 | Redirects to `midwesttaxstrategies.com`. Rebranded to "Midwest Tax Strategy", repositioned to *"Strategic Tax Planning for Established Real Estate Investors"* with *"$1M+ in assets"*, and states *"This is not reactive tax prep. This is strategy."* They have deliberately exited bookkeeping. |
| Molyet Bookkeeping & Accounting | 100 | Banner reads **"Grand Opening"**. A new practice has spare capacity, not overflow. |
| Parry's Bookkeeping | 100 | Domain does not resolve. |

**That is a 15% failure rate at the very top of the list.** The scoring model rates firmographic fit — name, title, services, geography — and cannot see a rebrand, a grand opening, or a dead domain.

**For gauging the market, this is the number that matters most.** If 15% of the best-scoring records are unusable, the tail is worse. Any sizing built on 617 records should be discounted accordingly, and the discount is not small.

---

## 2. The "solo practice" premise is half right

The eponymous naming pattern is real: Rea, Merritt, Hale, Parry, Molyet, Ulloa, Santucho, Campbell, Cavazos, Carter, Olson, Hall, Anderson, Ott. Fourteen of twenty carry the owner's surname.

**The solo inference behind it does not survive the site visits.**

- **Merritt Bookkeeping has two named owners** — Perry Paolini and Jordan Merritt, both on the homepage — plus *"thousands of clients"* and repeated references to a team.
- **"Our team" or "Meet the Team" appears on** Capstone, Carter & Company, Cavazos, In The Black, Santucho, Ulloa, Campbell, and Personalized Bookkeeping.

An earlier version of this report claimed 18 of 20 were solo practices. That was inferred from names and it was wrong. A firm named after its founder is not the same as a firm staffed by one person.

This matters because the pitch is different. A lone operator drowning is one story. A two-partner firm serving thousands of clients at a fixed $250/month is a margin story, and it needs different language.

## 4. The list is four markets, not one

This is the central finding. Sorting the 17 surviving accounts by what they actually do:

### A. Capacity-trapped delivery firms — the real overflow buyer

The thesis works here, and it works well.

- **Mattingly & Ott** advertises *"100% On-time delivery"* across *"40+ Client books managed"*, promises financials *"in your inbox by the 30th"*, and separately sells **Cleanup Bookkeeping** — *"we rebuild messy books from scratch"*. Cleanup is lumpy and unpredictable; it lands in the same weeks as the monthly close they have publicly guaranteed. The collision is structural and visible on their own homepage.
- **Christina Rea** publishes her rate card: cleanup at **$125/hour** against monthly retainers from **$225/month**. The economics push her to sell cleanup, and her own hours are the only place the hours come from.
- **Hale Bookkeeping** charges a **fixed monthly fee** to real estate investors, a client type that adds properties. Fixed price against growing scope.

**Roughly 3 to 4 of 17.** Small, but these are high-quality: the pain is observable rather than assumed, and two of them already sell the exact service Fenton would supply.

### B. Volume-and-margin firms — same offer, different pitch

**Merritt Bookkeeping**: two owners, *"thousands of clients"*, fixed **$250/month**, *"Uses QuickBooks — the industry standard"*, and a productised review process built on their own spreadsheet template. Not exhausted, engineered. High volume at a low fixed price is a unit-economics squeeze, not a time squeeze.

**1 of 17 in the sample, but likely the most scalable segment in the full 617** — productised low-price firms are exactly who buys wholesale capacity.

### C. Referral partners who do not run monthly books

**The Anderson Accounting and Advising Group** sells one-off advice sessions, a **"QuickBooks Set Up"** package of three 90-minute sessions, and a CFO Partnership. It has a shopping cart. They set clients up on QuickBooks Online and coach them. Nothing indicates they run the books afterwards.

**Smartup Accounting** states on its own services page: *"Many of our clients already have their own CPA and payroll specialist. If you don't, we'll get you connected with someone who can help you."*

These firms are not overflow buyers. **They are a referral channel**, and the trust objection is pre-answered because routing clients elsewhere is already what they do.

**2 of 17, and this is the most interesting discovery in the pass.**

### D. Tax-led practices that are not bookkeeping shops

**DLH Tax & Bookkeeping** — *"Active member since 1997"*, a "Partners Profile" page, tax services and business consulting. An established multi-partner tax practice. **Bowman Bookkeeping** — *"Your Tax Professional"*, tax-advice-led, no QuickBooks mention. Several of the "our team" CPA firms likely belong here too.

Bookkeeping is a line item, not the business. Weak fit for overflow bookkeeping capacity.

**Plausibly 5 or more of 17**, though this needs confirming per firm.

## Recommendations

**1. Do not size this market off 617 records.** Apply at least a 15% haircut for decay, and expect worse in the tail than at the head. A verification pass is cheap relative to sending into dead and rebranded firms.

**2. Segment B is probably the bigger prize, and it is untested.** Productised firms charging a fixed low monthly rate to many clients — the Merritt shape — have a structural reason to buy wholesale capacity that does not depend on anyone being overwhelmed. Their margin improves the moment delivery costs less. That is a more durable buying reason than exhaustion, and it is easier to talk about without implying they are failing.

**3. Multi-person firms should stay in the campaign but not in the same sequence.** They are not disqualified — a two-partner firm with thousands of clients has more work to place, not less. But the overflow language will land wrong. Split them and pitch unit economics instead. Do not research them further under the overflow thesis; that wastes the pass.

**4. Test the referral channel before scaling outreach.** Firms that set clients up on QuickBooks and hand them off need a bookkeeper on the other side. One referral partner can produce more than one overflow client, it requires no capacity pain to exist, and the objection Miriam worries about — poaching — is structurally absent because these firms are not keeping the delivery relationship anyway. Ask Smartup and Anderson directly. Two conversations.

**5. Drop the tax-led practices from this list.** Where bookkeeping is one service among tax, consulting and advisory, there is no bookkeeping capacity problem to solve. They inflate the list size and depress the response rate.

**6. Verify QuickBooks before claiming it.** Only 3 of 20 carry a ProAdvisor status. Either finish the ProAdvisor cross-reference against the full 617 or stop describing the list as QuickBooks-native.

**7. If only one thing gets done: talk to Mattingly & Ott.** They publish a 100% on-time promise across 40+ client books with a hard monthly deadline, and they sell cleanup rebuilds alongside it. Whether they say yes or no, the answer tells you whether the overflow thesis is real, because no account on this list has a clearer version of the problem.
