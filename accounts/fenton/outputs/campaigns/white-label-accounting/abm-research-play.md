# White-Label ABM play — the capacity research campaign

*Designed 2026-07-25. Not approved, not started. This is the concrete version of the ABM approach so it can be judged on specifics rather than on whether it sounds good.*

> **Read `strategy.md` first. It is the strategy and this file is subordinate to it.** If the two disagree, strategy.md wins. In particular the strategy sets the offer (QuickBooks cleanup and overflow, sold against the firm's own overload risk), the buying window (autumn, before busy season), and the two fears the buyer actually has. The sequence below is a way of getting into the room, not a reason to be there.

---

## The one-line version

Ask 40 small CPA firm owners how they are covering bookkeeping capacity, publish what they say, and let the firms who describe a capacity problem become the conversation. **The research question and the sales qualification question are the same question**, which is what stops this being a detour.

---

## Why this artifact and not a "TOP 100" list

The course example used an ego-flattering list (TOP100 ecommerce managers). That is the wrong instrument here.

CPA firm owners are conservative professionals in a licensed trade. A "top 100 bookkeepers" list from an unknown company reads as vanity spam, and Fenton has no publishing credibility to make it land. Worse, it flatters people rather than qualifying them, so a yes tells you nothing about whether they need anything.

**A capacity benchmark works instead, for three reasons:**

1. **The pain is real and current.** The accounting profession has a well-documented staffing shortage, and small firms feel it hardest because they cannot outbid regional firms for staff. This is not a manufactured hook.
2. **Firm owners are isolated and genuinely curious what peers do.** A small-practice owner rarely gets to compare notes on how anyone else handles busy season. Seeing the aggregate answer is worth 20 minutes to them.
3. **Every answer is qualification data.** A firm that says "we turned away two cleanup clients in March" has just described the exact problem Fenton solves, in their own words, without being pitched.

---

## What actually gets produced

**A short written piece. Not a podcast, not an event, not a PDF magazine.**

Roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words: what small firms said about covering bookkeeping capacity, what they tried, what worked. Anonymised by default, named only with permission. Published somewhere durable, and sent to every participant first.

Keep it small on purpose. A podcast needs an audience Fenton does not have. A physical publication needs budget Fenton does not have. A written summary needs one person and a few evenings, and it is the smallest artifact that makes the ask honest.

### The integrity condition, which is not negotiable

**If the piece does not get published, this is a lie, and it is a lie told under Miriam's name and her certification.** These are licensed professionals in a small world who talk to each other. A fake research request that goes nowhere is worse than no outreach at all, because it damages the one asset the whole strategy rests on.

Do not send the first invite until there is a real commitment to write and publish the thing, even if only 6 people participate. If that commitment is not there, run a different play.

## Who does what

This respects the standing division of labour: the operator owns outreach and the funnel, Miriam owns client conversation.

| Step | Who | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Build the target set, send invites, manage replies | **Operator** | It is outreach, and it protects Miriam's time |
| Conduct the interviews | **Operator** | Interviewing does not require being an accountant, it requires good questions. Introduce as "I work with Miriam Fenton, a QuickBooks ProAdvisor with 30 years in the books" |
| Write and publish the piece | **Operator** | Same reason |
| Any conversation that turns commercial | **Miriam** | The moment a firm asks what this would cost or how it would work, it is a client conversation and it is hers |
| Approve the framing before anything sends | **Miriam** | Her name is on it |

**The tradeoff of the operator running interviews:** less credibility in the room than Miriam would have, and no ability to go deep on a technical answer. The gain is that it costs Miriam almost nothing until a real opportunity exists, and it keeps the pipeline from competing with delivery. If interviews consistently stall on technical ground, revisit.

## The sequence

All LinkedIn. Copy below is drafted against `context/positioning.md` Copy Rules and still needs a linter pass before use.

### Touch 1 — connection request with note (300 character cap)

> Hi [First], I am putting together a short piece on how small firms are covering bookkeeping capacity in busy season. You have run [Firm] for a while so your read would be worth having. Open to 20 minutes?

*(205 characters. Names them, names the piece, gives a reason it is them, asks one small thing. No pitch, nothing about Fenton's services.)*

### Touch 2 — after they accept, sent as a normal message

> Thanks for connecting. The piece is short: I am asking around 40 small firm owners the same few questions about how they cover bookkeeping work when capacity gets tight, and writing up what comes back. Anonymous unless you would rather be named. Participants see it before anyone else. It takes about 20 minutes on a call, or I can send the questions in writing if that is easier. Would either work?

*(Offering the written option matters. A meaningful share of firm owners will answer questions in writing who would never book a call.)*

### Touch 3 — one follow-up, then stop

> Following up once in case this got buried. If the timing is wrong, no problem at all, and I will send you the finished piece either way.

**Then stop.** No fourth touch. The relationship is worth more than the marginal reply, and these people share a professional community.

### After the interview

Send the piece when it is published. That is the entire follow-up. Firms that described a capacity problem get a separate, human note from **Miriam** at that point, referencing what they actually said.

## The interview questions

Six questions, ordered so the useful ones come after they are warm.

1. How do you handle bookkeeping-heavy clients when things get tight?
2. Have you turned work away in the last year? What kind?
3. Do you use any outside help now, and how has that gone?
4. **What would have to be true for you to trust an outside bookkeeper with a client's file?**
5. **What are you doing about the Desktop clients before support runs out?**
6. What would you tell a firm owner who is about to hire their first bookkeeper?

**Question 4 is the one that pays for the whole exercise.** It asks a prospect to state their own buying criteria, unprompted, before anyone has tried to sell them anything. Whatever the aggregate answer is, that is the White-Label positioning, written by the buyers.

Question 2 is the qualification trigger. "Yes, we turned work away" is the signal.

**Question 5 was added 2026-07-25** off the migration research and it may be the most commercially loaded question on the list. Desktop 2023 support ended 2026-05-31 and Desktop 2024 goes 2027-09-30, so every firm with Desktop clients has to move them and most will move them to QuickBooks Online. The work that implies, validating and reconciling a book of migrated files, is the sharpest version of the cleanup offer, and the answer qualifies a firm instantly. Do not steer this toward Xero: Xero's directory lists only 617 advisors in the whole United States, against 11,351 unique Intuit ProAdvisors across 197 metros, so almost no US firm is going that way. See `xero-migration-research.md`.

## Expected numbers

Rates below are assumptions, not measured. Treat the shape as more reliable than the digits.

| Stage | Rate | Monthly, from 40 invites |
|---|---|---|
| Invites sent (noted, paid tier) | — | 40 |
| Accept | ~30% | ~12 |
| Agree to interview | ~35% of accepts | ~4 |
| Describe a real capacity gap | ~50% of interviews | ~2 |
| Become a client | ~20-30% of those | ~0.5 |

**3 clients lands around month 4 to 6**, consuming roughly 150 to 250 contacts. That is consistent with the earlier independent estimate, and it confirms the 618-contact list is two to four times larger than this goal requires.

**Kill threshold, set in advance:** if the first **20 invites** produce fewer than **2 interviews**, the ask is not working. Change the artifact or the framing before spending more. Do not grind through 200 invites hoping it turns.

## What this costs

| Item | Cost | When |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn Sales Navigator or Premium | ~$70-99/mo | Only when this actually starts |
| HeyReach or any automation | ~$79/mo | **Not yet. Probably not ever at this size.** |
| Producing the piece | Operator time, a few evenings | During |
| Miriam's time | Approval up front, then only real opportunities | Throughout |

**Do not buy automation for this.** 40 invites a month is two a day, sent by hand in a few minutes. HeyReach solves a scale problem Fenton does not have, and it adds account risk to the one account the whole channel depends on. Revisit only if this works and volume needs to triple.

## Two things this changes about earlier decisions

**1. White-Label no longer has to wait for Bounce to produce references.** The standing sequencing decision was that Bounce must run first because White-Label needs proof points. That reasoning applies to a *pitch*. It does not apply to an interview request, because you are not asking anyone to buy or to trust you with client work. **This play can start before Fenton has a single testimonial**, which removes the Q4 dependency.

**2. It gives the Clay credits a better job than sourcing.** The highest-value enrichment now is not more firms, it is knowing which of the existing 618 are actually active on LinkedIn. The course material's filter is connection count: someone with 500+ connections built that deliberately and is likely to see a message. At 40 touches a month, picking the right 40 beats having another 600 names.
