**Sources**
- Matt McDonagh, *"What is Revenue Engineering?"*, Mastering Revenue Operations,
  18 April 2024 — **partially paywalled; roughly the first third is public.**
- Jake Bivens, *"Rise of the Revenue Engineer (vs. GTM Engineer)"*, QC Growth,
  13 January 2026.
- Doug Bell, *"Why We're Changing from GTM Engineers to Revenue Growth Engineers"*,
  Cannonball GTM, 31 January 2026.

---

## The thesis

The GTM engineer began as an **execution layer** — built lists, wired tools, made
outreach scale — and is being pulled upward into ownership of the revenue system itself.
All three describe that movement; none explains it the same way, and the disagreements
are more instructive than the agreement.

## The three accounts

**McDonagh (2024) — the revenue engineer as data architect.** Defined against RevOps, not
sales: RevOps keeps the existing engine running (routing, hygiene, campaign measurement,
troubleshooting); revenue engineering designs and rebuilds that engine for performance it
does not yet have. His analogy: RevOps in the pit lane, revenue engineering back at HQ
reading telemetry and redesigning the car. The work is infrastructural — what data is
captured and when, how it is stored, how it moves between CRM, marketing automation, and
billing — and the role is accountable for its integrity, because forecasting, lead
scoring, and churn prediction are all downstream of it.

**Bivens (2026) — convergence, and an operating mindset rather than a title.** RevOps
(systems, data, process) and GTM engineering (campaigns, lists, enrichment, tooling) have
blurred into one as revenue motions became technical. He credits the framing to Benjamin
Reed of RevyOps rather than claiming the term. His center of gravity is **signal**: the
hard part is not building the pipeline but knowing which signals indicate real readiness —
which requires understanding product, positioning, and history — then wiring source, list,
scoring, message, and channel into something that runs repeatedly. A strong signal with
weak messaging fails; so does good messaging aimed at the wrong list. The role must
*build*, not only operate. **His closing position is the strongest ownership statement of
the three: the revenue engineer owns no single channel or tool, but the system — signals,
prioritization, messaging, channels, feedback, and the automation underneath all of it.**

**Bell (2026) — a deliberate rename**, GTM Engineer → Revenue Growth Engineer. His
objection to the old title is the thesis: it implies acquisition only — the meeting, the
demo, top of funnel — while the strongest practitioners work across expansion, territory
design, and account research. *Revenue* = accountability for pipeline, win rates, and
expansion dollars rather than send volumes. *Growth* = new logos plus retention and
upsell. *Engineer* = writing code, not operating no-code workflows. He predicts a barbell:
revenue growth engineers at one end, brand marketers at the other, traditional product
marketing and demand gen compressed out of the middle. His trigger is capability, dated to
~90 days — a colleague building three production-grade applications live on stage in under
two hours.

## Where they diverge

| | McDonagh | Bivens | Bell |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What the role is** | A discipline above RevOps | An operating mindset, explicitly not a title | A job title, renamed on purpose |
| **Where the constraint sits** | The data model | Signal selection and its wiring | Build speed and code fluency |
| **Relation to RevOps** | Distinct and complementary; both needed | Merging into one role | Largely absent; positioned against marketing |
| **What triggered the shift** | Data maturity; no AI framing | Gradual convergence of two disciplines | Coding agents, ~90 days |
| **Scope of ownership** | Infrastructure the engine runs on | End-to-end system, into CS and marketing | Full revenue lifecycle including expansion |

**The most useful row is the trigger.** McDonagh, writing two years earlier, needs no AI
to justify the role — his case rests entirely on data architecture. If the thesis were
purely a story about coding agents, his article would not exist. **The pull toward
ownership predates the tooling; the tooling accelerated a movement already underway.**

## What all three agree on

1. **Scope has outgrown acquisition.** Each defines the role against a narrower
   predecessor — RevOps maintenance, campaign execution, top-of-funnel — and expands it
   toward the full revenue lifecycle.
2. **Accountability moves from activity to outcome.** Bell states it most directly
   (rejecting send volumes as a metric); Bivens' list of what the role answers for and
   McDonagh's tying forecasting quality to data design make the same move.
3. **Building is part of the job.** All three require producing systems that do not come
   out of the box.
4. **The system must close a loop.** Feedback is a design requirement, not reporting —
   results return to where teams work, or the intelligence is inert.

## Testing the thesis — the operator's own limits

**What holds.** Three independent writers, two years apart, converge on the same
directional claim without borrowing each other's vocabulary. Bivens explicitly declines to
claim the term and points to a fourth source, which suggests the idea is arriving from
several directions rather than being pushed by one publisher. Reasonable evidence of a
real shift rather than a naming fashion.

**What does not, yet.** **All three describe capability, not authority.** Owning a system
is not owning a number, and none shows an organization where compensation, headcount, or
forecast accountability actually sits with the role. Bell comes closest by naming pipeline
and expansion dollars as the measures, but his evidence is a capability demonstration —
software built fast on a stage — which proves the person can build, not that the business
handed over the outcome. **Two of the three are written by agencies whose services are the
role in question**, so the incentive to declare it inevitable is worth holding in view.

**The honest version.** The execution layer is absorbing more of the revenue system than
it used to, and the ceiling on what one technical operator can build has risen sharply.
Whether that becomes *ownership* depends on something none of these articles evidence:
whether companies restructure accountability to match, or simply expect more output from
the same seat.

**One case where the question is already closed [operator-derived, 08-14]:** for a
founder-operator, capability and authority are the same person — the falsifiers below
are enterprise questions, and this operation is a case where the merger is already fact.

## What would settle it — the falsifiers

- Job postings attaching quota, pipeline, or NRR targets to the title rather than activity
  or delivery targets.
- Reporting lines — into revenue leadership, or still inside marketing/sales ops.
- Whether the barbell prediction shows up as actual compression of demand-gen and product-
  marketing headcount.
- Whether RevOps converges with the role (Bivens) or persists alongside it (McDonagh).

**These are the kit's upgrade conditions.** Until one is observed, the definition in
`revenue-engineering.md` is a directional claim the operator has adopted, not an
established fact about how companies are organized.
